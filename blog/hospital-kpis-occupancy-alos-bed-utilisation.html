Hospital KPIs Explained: Occupancy, ALOS, Discharge Time and Bed Utilisation

Hospital KPIs help leaders understand how well services and resources are working. Bed occupancy, average length of stay, discharge turnaround time and bed utilisation can reveal operational patterns, but each measure needs a clear definition and the right context.

A KPI is useful when leaders know what it measures, where the data comes from, who reviews it and what action may follow. No single occupancy or ALOS target applies to every hospital.

What are hospital KPIs?

Hospital KPIs, or key performance indicators, are measures used to monitor selected areas of hospital performance. They can help leaders review patient flow, bed capacity, service delivery, staffing, quality and financial performance.

India's Ministry of Health and Family Welfare includes indicators such as bed occupancy, ALOS and cancelled surgeries in its Indian Public Health Standards for district hospitals. Hospitals should use the definitions and requirements that apply to their own type of institution and operating context.

1. Bed occupancy rate

Bed occupancy rate estimates how much of a hospital's available bed capacity is occupied over a defined period.

A commonly used formula is:

Bed occupancy rate = occupied patient bed days ÷ available bed days × 100

For a monthly calculation, available bed days are usually calculated using the number of functional or available beds multiplied by the number of days in the period. The MoHFW's IPHS formula uses functional beds and calendar days.

What can occupancy tell hospital leaders?

Occupancy can help leaders consider:

  • Whether beds are being used in line with demand
  • Whether particular wards or services face capacity pressure
  • Whether staffed, functional beds match the number of beds reported
  • Whether admission and discharge processes need review

What occupancy cannot tell you by itself

A high occupancy rate does not automatically mean the hospital is performing well. Leaders need to consider service mix, bed availability, patient flow and safe capacity. A low rate also needs context: it may reflect demand, staffing, service availability or how bed capacity has been defined.

The WHO advises that bed occupancy should be interpreted alongside other information. It also cautions that there is no single occupancy level that is optimal for every situation.

2. Average length of stay (ALOS)

Average length of stay estimates how long inpatients stay in the hospital on average during a defined period.

A common formula is:

ALOS = inpatient bed days ÷ inpatient discharges during the same period

The exact counting rules should be documented. For example, the MoHFW's IPHS calculation specifies which discharges to include and excludes newborns from patient bed days in its district-hospital reporting format.

How should leaders interpret ALOS?

ALOS is best reviewed alongside:

  • Specialty and case mix
  • Patient outcomes and readmissions, where relevant
  • Discharge planning and delays
  • Service capacity and care pathways
  • Trends over time using consistent definitions

A shorter stay is not automatically better. A delay in treatment or discharge may affect ALOS, but shortening a stay without considering the patient's needs would not be a sound management goal. The WHO notes that ALOS varies with the cases and care provided and should be interpreted with other information.

3. Discharge turnaround time

Discharge turnaround time measures the time taken for a defined discharge process to move from a specified start point to a specified end point.

Hospitals may use different start and end points, so the definition should be explicit. For example, a hospital might measure from the recorded discharge decision to the patient leaving the bed, while also tracking separate process stages such as documentation, billing and pharmacy clearance.

The chosen measure should:

  • Use reliable timestamps
  • Clarify which patient groups or discharge types are included
  • Separate delays by stage where possible
  • Be reviewed with the relevant department teams
  • Protect appropriate clinical decision-making

A discharge time KPI should support better coordination and visibility. It should not encourage a discharge before the responsible clinical team considers it appropriate.

4. Bed utilisation

"Bed utilisation" is sometimes used to mean bed occupancy, and sometimes used more broadly to describe whether bed capacity is available and being used effectively.

To avoid confusion, a hospital should state exactly what its measure means. It should clarify:

  • Which beds count as available: installed, functional, staffed or open beds
  • Which patient days are included
  • The reporting period and census method
  • Whether closed beds or temporary capacity changes are excluded

If two departments use different definitions, their figures may not be comparable. Documenting the numerator and denominator makes trends more useful.

Comparing the four measures

KPIWhat it describesUseful follow-up question
Bed occupancy rateOccupied bed days compared with available bed daysDo we have the right number of functional and staffed beds for demand?
ALOSAverage inpatient stay over a defined periodWhat explains the change, and how does it vary by specialty or case mix?
Discharge turnaround timeTime taken across a defined discharge processWhich stage is contributing most to delay?
Bed utilisationHow hospital-defined bed capacity is being usedAre the bed definitions and data consistent across services?

How should hospitals use these KPIs together?

These measures are more useful when reviewed together than in isolation. For example, if occupancy changes, leaders can examine bed availability, admissions and discharges. If ALOS changes, they can review case mix and patient pathways. If discharge turnaround time rises, they can identify which step is taking longer.

A practical review process should:

  • Confirm the indicator's definition and data source.
  • Compare it with the hospital's own previous periods using the same method.
  • Look for differences by department or service where appropriate.
  • Ask teams for operational context.
  • Agree on an action owner and a date to review progress.

The MoHFW's IPHS documents state that the guidelines are revised over time and allow flexibility for the needs of different states and regions. Hospitals should therefore confirm which current requirements apply to their setting.

Common KPI mistakes to avoid

  • Comparing figures calculated with different bed or discharge definitions
  • Setting the same target for hospitals with different specialties or operating models
  • Treating a change in one KPI as proof of a specific cause
  • Focusing on speed without considering patient needs and quality
  • Reporting a number without assigning anyone to review or act on it
  • Making decisions from incomplete or unverified data

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate hospital bed occupancy rate?

A common calculation is occupied patient bed days divided by available bed days, multiplied by 100. The hospital should document how it defines available beds and patient bed days.

What is ALOS in a hospital?

ALOS means average length of stay. It estimates the average time in hospital for inpatients over a defined period. The calculation and included discharge categories should be consistent and documented.

Is a high bed occupancy rate good?

Not necessarily. A high rate may indicate strong demand, but it should be considered alongside staffed bed availability, patient flow, service mix and the hospital's ability to provide appropriate care.

Is bed utilisation the same as bed occupancy?

Sometimes the terms are used similarly, but "bed utilisation" can be broader or defined differently by different hospitals. State the exact formula and the type of beds included.

What is a good discharge turnaround time?

There is no single figure that fits every hospital. Define the start and end points, study the time taken at each stage and review delays with the relevant teams. Do not use a speed target in a way that compromises appropriate discharge decisions.

How often should these hospital KPIs be reviewed?

The review frequency depends on the measure and the hospital's management process. Operational issues may need more frequent review than longer-term trends. Keep the definitions consistent so that comparisons remain meaningful.
