Hospital SOPs: How to Create, Implement and Review Them

Quick Answer

Hospital SOPs are controlled procedures that explain how staff should carry out defined processes. To work well, they need clear responsibilities, appropriate review and approval, staff access and training, and periodic review.

Hospital SOPs are written procedures that explain how staff should carry out defined processes in a hospital. They help make responsibilities, steps, records and escalation routes clear.

But writing an SOP is only the first step. For an SOP to be useful, it must reflect how the hospital intends work to be done, be approved by the appropriate people, be accessible to staff, and be reviewed when the process changes.

What is a hospital SOP?

An SOP, or standard operating procedure, is a controlled document that describes the agreed steps for carrying out a specific process.

A hospital may have SOPs for both clinical and non-clinical activities. Examples include:

  • Patient registration and appointment handling
  • Admission, transfer and discharge processes
  • Complaint receipt and escalation
  • Equipment checks and maintenance reporting
  • Inventory receipt and stock issue
  • Shift handover and department reporting
  • Staff induction and training records

The appropriate clinical leaders should review and approve clinical procedures. SOPs should also align with applicable hospital policies, requirements and accreditation standards.

SOP, policy or protocol: what is the difference?

These terms are related, but they do different jobs:

DocumentMain purpose
PolicyStates the hospital's position, rule or expectation on a subject
SOPExplains the steps and responsibilities for carrying out a process
Protocol or clinical guidelineProvides approved direction for a defined clinical situation

Hospitals may use these terms differently. The important thing is to define them consistently in the hospital's document-control system.

What should a hospital SOP include?

The format can vary, but a practical SOP commonly includes:

  • Title and document number
  • Purpose: Why the procedure exists
  • Scope: Which departments, roles or situations it applies to
  • Responsibilities: Who performs, checks and approves the work
  • Procedure: The steps, in the order they should happen
  • Escalation: What to do when the expected process cannot be followed
  • Records: Forms, registers or system entries to complete
  • References: Related policies, forms or standards, where applicable
  • Approval and review details: Owner, approver, version and review date

The steps should be clear enough for the intended staff to follow without relying on informal explanations.

How to create a hospital SOP

1. Choose a process that needs clarity

Start with a real operational need. The process may be causing repeated delays, inconsistent practice, confusion between departments, incomplete records or avoidable escalation.

Avoid writing documents simply to increase the size of the manual. Each SOP should have a defined purpose.

2. Observe how the process works

Speak with the staff who carry out the work and observe the process where appropriate. Identify the handovers, decisions, records and common exceptions.

This helps the hospital distinguish between the intended process and the way work is currently happening.

3. Define roles and sequence

Write the steps in the order they occur. Name the role responsible at each step, including who checks completion and who should be informed if the process is delayed or cannot be followed.

Avoid vague instructions such as "inform the concerned department." Specify the role or approved escalation route.

4. Draft in clear, practical language

Use short steps, familiar terms and clear actions. If the process needs a form, checklist or system entry, identify it by name. Use a flowchart only when it makes the sequence easier to understand.

5. Review with the right people

The process owner and affected departments should review the draft. Clinical SOPs should go through the hospital's appropriate clinical review and approval process.

The SOP should also be checked for conflicts with related policies, forms and existing workflows.

6. Approve, control and issue the document

Give each SOP a version, owner, approver and review date. Make the current version available to the staff who need it. Remove or clearly mark superseded versions so staff are not left choosing between conflicting instructions.

How to implement SOPs across departments and shifts

An SOP is not implemented just because it has been uploaded or circulated. Staff need to know where to find it, understand the steps that apply to their roles and have a way to ask questions.

Hospitals can support implementation by:

  • Introducing the SOP through role-based training
  • Explaining what has changed and when it takes effect
  • Using brief checklists or job aids at the point of work when helpful
  • Including the SOP in staff induction where relevant
  • Checking whether staff can access the current version
  • Reviewing implementation through supervision, records or internal audits

How often should hospital SOPs be reviewed?

Set a review period according to the hospital's document-control process and the importance of the procedure. Review an SOP earlier when:

  • The process, equipment or system changes
  • Responsibilities or department structures change
  • A recurring problem indicates the steps need attention
  • An audit or review identifies a gap
  • Applicable requirements change

A review should confirm that the document is still accurate, approved and usable by the staff expected to follow it.

Common mistakes when writing hospital SOPs

  • Copying a generic SOP without adapting it: The steps may not match the hospital's staffing or workflow.
  • Writing for auditors instead of users: Staff need practical steps, not only formal language.
  • Leaving responsibilities unclear: Steps can be missed when no role owns them.
  • Ignoring exceptions: Staff need a clear escalation route when the usual process cannot be followed.
  • Not training staff: Circulation alone does not confirm understanding.
  • Keeping old versions in circulation: Multiple versions can cause inconsistent practice.
  • Reviewing documents without observing work: A well-written SOP does not prove the process is working as intended.

A quick hospital SOP review checklist

Before issuing or revising an SOP, ask:

  • Does the title make the process easy to identify?
  • Is the purpose and scope clear?
  • Are roles and responsibilities named?
  • Are steps written in the order they happen?
  • Are handovers and escalation routes clear?
  • Are required forms and records identified?
  • Has the right process owner reviewed it?
  • Have required clinical or departmental approvals been obtained?
  • Can staff access the current version?
  • Is training or communication needed?
  • Is there an owner and review date?

Frequently asked questions

What is a hospital SOP manual?

A hospital SOP manual is a controlled collection of standard operating procedures for the organisation or selected departments. It should have a consistent document-control method so staff can find the current approved procedure.

Which SOPs should a hospital create first?

Start with processes that are unclear, frequently repeated, cross-departmental or linked to a known operational concern. Priorities will vary by hospital and should be agreed with relevant leaders.

Who should approve hospital SOPs?

Approval depends on the SOP's subject and the hospital's governance arrangements. Clinical procedures should be reviewed through the appropriate clinical leadership process; operational procedures should be approved by the relevant process owner and designated authority.

How do hospitals ensure staff follow SOPs?

Make current SOPs accessible, explain them through role-based training, clarify responsibilities and review how the process is being carried out. Use findings to improve both the procedure and its implementation.

How is an SOP different from a checklist?

An SOP describes the agreed procedure and responsibilities. A checklist helps staff confirm that selected steps or items have been completed. A checklist may support an SOP but does not replace it.
